## Problem Statement

Kids desperately want to be creators — they copy their favorite YouTubers, say
"like and subscribe," want to be stars. Parents want to say yes. But every
available platform (YouTube, TikTok, Instagram) makes "yes" feel like negligence:
algorithms, predators, surveillance, monetization of your child's attention.

So parents say no. The demand is suppressed, not displaced. These families aren't
using a competitor — they're just living with the guilt of blocking their kid's
creative expression.

TubeStr is the only platform that makes "yes" structurally safe: kids create,
families watch, no corporation can see it. Architecture > policy.

## Status Quo

There is no adequate workaround. Parents just say no.

- YouTube: algorithms, predators, monetization. "No."
- TikTok: worse. "No."
- Private YouTube channels: still Google. Still surveillance. "No."
- WhatsApp family groups: clips in chat threads, no kid-facing interface. "No."

The cost of the status quo is the kid's creative expression, and the parent's guilt
about blocking it. That's a real cost, even if it doesn't show up in a spreadsheet.

## Premises

1. The real problem is suppressed creative expression, not missing privacy features.
   Parents want to say YES. TubeStr is the infrastructure that makes yes possible.

2. Parker's toy kitchen video and Cooper's sleepover are real demand evidence —
   not just for the founder's family, but because Cooper's friends were third-party
   users who engaged without any pitch.

3. Bitcoin Jungle is the natural first market: tech-savvy, privacy-conscious, zero
   education overhead, physically accessible to the founder.

4. The growth loop (kid creates → shares with circle → someone asks "what app is
   this?") is now testable because the app works end-to-end.

5. Nostr/MLS is the moat for competitors — but the pitch to parents is simpler:
   "we can't see your kids' videos" is better than "we won't." Architecture is the
   proof. It's not the headline.
